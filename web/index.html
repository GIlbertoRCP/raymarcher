Physics Guide

Gravitational Lensing: The strong gravitational field of the black hole warps spacetime, forcing photons to follow curved paths. Light from behind the black hole is focused toward the observer, producing visual double images, distorted arches, and circular Einstein Rings.

Accretion Disk: Gas and dust orbiting the black hole form a thin, spinning disk. Because light is lensed, you can see both the top and bottom of the rear side of the disk, warped into halos that wrap around the event horizon.

Doppler Beaming: The side of the accretion disk rotating toward you appears significantly brighter and hotter (blueshifted) due to relativistic velocity. The side rotating away is dimmer and redder (redshifted).

How this works

This renders a non-rotating Schwarzschild black hole by tracing one light ray per pixel backwards from the camera through curved spacetime.

1. Bending of light

In isotropic coordinates, light in Schwarzschild spacetime behaves like light in a medium whose refractive index grows near the black hole. Each ray is stepped with a 4th-order Runge-Kutta (RK4) integrator.

n(r) = (1 + u)³ / (1 − u), u = M / 2r

Units are geometric (G = c = 1). The horizon sits at isotropic r = M/2, which is the areal radius R = 2M.

2. Accretion disk

The disk is a volumetric emitter with a Gaussian vertical profile and procedural turbulence. Its density, temperature profile and edges are artistic choices, not a physical disk model.

3. Doppler and gravitational shifts

Gas moving toward the camera looks hotter and brighter; light climbing out of the gravity well loses energy. Both are applied to the disk colour.

g = g_doppler × g_grav, T_observed = g × T_emitted

The current Doppler factor uses the gas velocity in flat-space form; a fully covariant version is planned.

4. What's next

The physics here is being replaced by a spacetime compiler that generates the ray equations for any metric, including spinning (Kerr) black holes and wormholes.
