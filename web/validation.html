Plates Verification

Verification

Every image in this atlas is computed from a spacetime's metric, one light ray per pixel. This page shows the checks that say it is computed correctly: measurements against results published in the physics literature, and a second, independent renderer that has to produce the same picture.

The ledger

Each line is one measurement, the tolerance it had to meet, and the result. The reference integrator runs in double precision on an NVIDIA RTX 3070; the interactive integrator is the one the plates use.

Two backends, one image

The same compiled metric drives two tracers that share no code: WebGPU in your browser and CUDA on the desktop. Rendered from the same scene files, every pixel is sorted into what the light did: it fell into the hole, landed on the disk, or escaped to the sky. Away from the edges between those regions, where one pixel can straddle both, the two must agree on at least 99.5% of pixels.

Fate map: escaped to the sky · landed on the disk · fell in. Each tile is the CUDA render; the score is its agreement with WebGPU.

    Bending of light

    A ray passing a Schwarzschild black hole at impact parameter b is bent by an angle that has an exact answer as an integral. Far away it approaches Einstein's 4M/b; close in, near b = √27 M ≈ 5.196 M, the ray winds around the hole and the angle grows without bound.

    The size of the shadow

    A camera hovering at radius r sees a dark disk of angular radius α, with sin α = √27 M √(1 − 2M/r) / r. The tracer finds the edge by bisecting on whether rays fall in.

    A spinning hole's shadow

    Spin flattens one side of the shadow and shifts it sideways: light orbiting with the spin can pass closer. James Bardeen worked out the outline in 1973. The tracer launches parallel rays from a million M away and bisects for the edge along each of Bardeen's points.

    Orbits

    Two radii shape every black hole image: the photon orbit, where light can circle the hole, and the innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO), where the disk ends. Both have exact formulas for Kerr. Photon orbits are found by tracing light; the ISCO from the metric's own derivatives, evaluated on the GPU.

    How the integrator behaves

    The plates use fixed-step fourth-order Runge–Kutta (RK4): halve the step and the error should drop sixteen-fold. The reference integrator, Dormand–Prince RK45, picks its own steps to meet a requested tolerance.

    Keeping light on the light cone

    For light, H = ½ gμν pμ pν is exactly zero. Numerical error lets it drift, so its size along each ray is a free accuracy check on every pixel of a frame.

    Speed

    Each pixel integrates a whole geodesic, so a 1080p frame is about two million small physics simulations. Timings are for the trace only, on one RTX 3070, with identical scenes in both backends.
